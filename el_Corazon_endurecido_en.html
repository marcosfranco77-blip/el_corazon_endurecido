The Hardness of the Heart

A journey to understand — and heal — our heart's sensitivity toward God

Imagine this: you're reading quietly through your Bible when you come to a familiar story — Jesus walking on the water in the middle of a storm, in front of His terrified disciples. To really understand the scene, it helps to remember what had happened just hours before.

That same day, Jesus had fed a crowd of at least five thousand men (likely more than fifteen thousand people counting women and children) using only five loaves and two fish. When it was over, there was more food left than there had been at the start — a miracle the disciples witnessed with their own eyes, handing out the food themselves.

After this, Jesus sent His disciples ahead in a boat while He stayed behind to pray on the mountain. Night fell, a strong storm rose on the Sea of Galilee — a lake surrounded by mountains where storms can form violently within minutes — and the disciples, most of them experienced fishermen, were straining hard against the wind. Then:

"Then He saw them straining at rowing, for the wind was against them. Now about the fourth watch of the night He came to them, walking on the sea, and would have passed them by. And when they saw Him walking on the sea, they supposed it was a ghost, and cried out; for they all saw Him and were troubled. But immediately He talked with them and said to them, 'Be of good cheer! It is I; do not be afraid.' Then He went up into the boat to them, and the wind ceased. And they were greatly amazed in themselves beyond measure, and marveled."

Mark 6:48-51, NKJV

And then comes the verse that changes everything:

"…for they had not understood about the loaves, because their heart was hardened."

Mark 6:52, NKJV

Pause for a moment on that phrase. Scripture doesn't say the disciples were unbelieving, or rebellious, or living in sin. It says their heart was hardened — and that's why they couldn't process what they were seeing. Just hours earlier, they had watched Jesus multiply loaves out of nothing. And yet, seeing Him walk on water left them stunned, as if they'd never witnessed anything supernatural in their lives.

That verse is the doorway into everything we're about to walk through together. Because if this happened to the disciples — men who walked physically with Jesus — it's worth asking: could the same thing be happening to us?

Point 1

Understanding what a hardened heart is

Not what you might think

When we hear the phrase "hardened heart," the mind usually goes to one place: someone in outright rebellion against God, a cold and hostile heart, a declared enemy of the faith. But Scripture shows us something different — and, frankly, more unsettling.

The word "hardened" simply means cold, unfeeling, inflexible. Applied to the heart, it describes something very specific: being more connected to the natural than to the supernatural. It's not two separate hearts — one "physical" and one "spiritual" — but one and the same heart, which can be more accustomed to trusting what can be seen, touched, and explained by human logic than what God says and does.

Think, for example, of someone who can recall every detail of their favorite football teams and players — lineups, stats, results from ten years ago — yet who can't recall or apply what God has already done in their own life. It's not that this person has "two hearts," one open and one closed. It's one heart, fully capable of sensitivity and memory, that simply has never been pointed, with that same attention, toward the things of God.

Under this definition, hardness of heart stops being a problem exclusive to "bad people." It becomes a condition that can coexist perfectly with a morally upright life. You can avoid foul language, never drink, never smoke, never touch pornography — and still have a heart that's hardened in certain areas toward God. The clearest symptom isn't obvious sin: it's being utterly astonished, caught completely off guard, when God does something supernatural. If God's power takes us entirely by surprise, that's a sign that, deep down, we were expecting something else — betting, without realizing it, that the natural would ultimately decide the outcome.

The symptoms, in Jesus's own words

A few days after the first multiplication of loaves, something almost identical happened: Jesus fed another crowd, this time four thousand people, with only seven loaves (Mark 8:1-9). In other words, the disciples had a second miracle of the same kind fresh in their memory. Yet shortly after, traveling by boat and realizing they'd forgotten to bring bread, they began to worry — as if Jesus couldn't solve that problem. It was at that moment Jesus spoke to them plainly:

"But Jesus, being aware of it, said to them, 'Why do you reason because you have no bread? Do you not yet perceive nor understand? Is your heart still hardened? Having eyes, do you not see? And having ears, do you not hear? And do you not remember?'"

Mark 8:17-18, NKJV

That question lays out, one after another, the symptoms of a hard heart:

  • Cannot perceive
  • Cannot understand
  • Cannot see (spiritually)
  • Cannot hear (God's voice)
  • Cannot remember

Notice that none of these symptoms have anything to do with intelligence or mental capacity. They're incapacities of the spiritual realm. A person can have an incredible memory for sports, politics, or work figures, and yet easily "forget" what God has already done in their own life. That's not a memory problem — it's a problem of where the heart is truly fixed. We remember, without effort, what genuinely matters to us; what we "forget" almost always reveals, more than a mental lapse, a lack of interest underneath.

Two biblical mirrors of this condition

So this truth doesn't stay in the abstract, Scripture gives us two vivid portraits — both well known, but worth revisiting in more detail than we usually give them.

The first is Pharaoh. When God sent Moses to demand the release of the people of Israel, who had been enslaved for generations in Egypt, Pharaoh refused again and again, even in the face of ten devastating plagues. The book of Exodus explicitly mentions, fifteen times, that his heart was "hardened." What's striking isn't just that he refused to obey, but how he reasoned while doing it.

When the plague of frogs literally overran his food, his bed, and his shoes, Pharaoh humbled himself and called Moses to ask for relief:

"So Moses said to Pharaoh, 'Accept the honor of saying when I shall intercede for you… that the frogs may be destroyed from you… they shall remain in the river only.' So he said, 'Tomorrow.'"

Exodus 8:9-10, NKJV, abridged

Think about that: able to ask for immediate relief, Pharaoh chose to wait one more day — while literally surrounded by frogs. That's the defining mark of a hardened heart: not the lack of evidence, but the inability to reason correctly even with the evidence right in front of you. Later, when the plagues had already devastated much of Egypt, even Pharaoh's own officials confronted him:

"Then Pharaoh's servants said to him, 'How long shall this man be a snare to us? Let the men go, that they may serve the LORD their God. Do you not yet know that Egypt is destroyed?'"

Exodus 10:7, NKJV

And still, Pharaoh answered, furious, with a death threat against Moses:

"Then Pharaoh said to him, 'Get away from me! Take heed to yourself and see my face no more! For in the day you see my face you shall die!'"

Exodus 10:28, NKJV

Note

One important clarification here: under the new covenant — that is, for us today — God does not harden anyone's heart.

The second portrait is the people of Israel in the wilderness. After leaving Egypt, they crossed the Red Sea on dry ground as the waters parted before them, watched nine devastating plagues fall on their oppressors, and were led by day in a pillar of cloud and by night in a pillar of fire. Yet within just two or three days, when food began to run short in the desert, they were already wondering whether God could feed such a multitude. The book of Hebrews, written centuries later, uses this very episode as a warning to believers:

"Therefore, as the Holy Spirit says: 'Today, if you will hear His voice, do not harden your hearts as in the rebellion, in the day of trial in the wilderness, where your fathers tested Me, tried Me, and saw My works forty years.'"

Hebrews 3:7-9, NKJV

Even the book of Job uses a curious image, drawn from the animal world, to illustrate this same condition: the ostrich, which buries her eggs in the sand carelessly, leaving them exposed to be trampled by any passing animal:

"She treats her young harshly, as though they were not hers; her labor is in vain, without concern, because God deprived her of wisdom, and did not endow her with understanding."

Job 39:16-17, NKJV

The comparison is harsh, but precise: hardness of heart isn't simple stubbornness — it is, literally, a lack of wisdom and understanding, even in creatures (or people) who could know better.

A first pause to reflect

If we've made it this far and something inside you feels uncomfortable, that's a good sign — not of condemnation, but that your heart is still sensitive to this conversation. The question worth asking isn't "do I have a hardened heart?" but something more specific: "in which concrete areas of my life am I more surprised by the natural than by the supernatural? Where do I react first with human logic, and only afterward — if at all — with faith?"

With that question in mind, let's move to the second leg of this journey: understanding where this condition really comes from.

Point 2

Where hardness of heart comes from

Before we go further, it's worth stating this clearly, because it holds up everything that follows: it is not God who hardens a person's heart toward Him. It's the person who, little by little, allows their own heart to harden. The one recorded exception in all of Scripture — Pharaoh — happened under a different covenant than the one we live under today; for those of us who live under grace, hardness of heart always starts in the heart itself, never in God.

The surprise: it's not mainly sin

It's tempting to think hardness of heart is simply synonymous with living in sin. And yes, sin does harden — Scripture says so plainly, in that same Hebrews passage we quoted earlier:

"but exhort one another daily, while it is called 'Today,' lest any of you be hardened through the deceitfulness of sin."

Hebrews 3:13, NKJV

But here's the central turn of this whole journey: sin is not the most important cause, nor the most common one. Go back to the disciples in the boat. They weren't in rebellion, weren't looking at anything indecent, weren't arguing or sinning in any recognizable way — they were simply struggling to survive a storm, doing exactly what any experienced fisherman would do in their place. And still, their heart was hardened.

So what caused it? The answer is in a single word that captures it best: consider.

"…for they had not understood about the loaves, because their heart was hardened."

Mark 6:52, NKJV

Note

The older King James Version renders this verse even more directly — "for they considered not the miracle of the loaves." That word, "considered," is the key that unlocks everything that follows in this teaching.

To "consider" means to study, weigh, reflect on, meditate on, focus on something in a sustained way — not just to notice it in passing. The disciples had just watched five loaves and two fish feed thousands of people — but they didn't stop to meditate on it, to let that truth really sink into how they thought. They saw it, lived it, even handed out the food with their own hands — and still didn't "consider" it in the deep sense of the word. So when the next supernatural act came, it caught them completely off guard, as if the first miracle had never happened.

Here we arrive at the principle that holds up everything else:

Whatever you sustain your attention on is what ends up dominating how you think and react. And whatever you don't give your attention to, no matter how real or important it is, will not shape your heart.

Read that again, slowly. It's not an empty motivational line: it is, quite simply, the explanation for why the heart grows sensitive toward some things and hardened toward others. And notice something important: this doesn't describe a fight. It's not that you have to force your attention toward God while actively battling every contrary thought one by one. It's simpler — and deeper — than that: it's a law, as automatic as sowing and reaping. What we meditate on, over time, becomes the lens through which we interpret everything else, without our having to fight for it. What we neglect, even something we've lived through firsthand, fades on its own, until it loses its power to influence us.

How a temptation is really born

To understand this more deeply, it's worth looking at how Scripture describes the very birth of a temptation — because the same mechanism that hardens the heart toward the supernatural is what causes a person to fall into sin:

"But each one is tempted when he is drawn away by his own desires and enticed. Then, when desire has conceived, it gives birth to sin; and sin, when it is full-grown, brings forth death."

James 1:14-15, NKJV

Notice the language: desire, conceived, gives birth. It's the exact same vocabulary we'd use to describe a pregnancy. And the implication is huge: just as a baby can't be born without first being conceived, a sin — or a doubt, or unbelief — cannot show up in a person's life without first being "conceived" in their mind — that is, without having been thought, imagined, considered with some degree of attention. No one can be tempted by something they've never had the opportunity to think about.

This is seen with total clarity in the account of the fall of man, in Genesis 3. For context: Adam and Eve lived in the Garden of Eden, with only one restriction — don't eat from the tree of the knowledge of good and evil. The serpent (later identified in Scripture as the devil) approaches Eve, not with a direct physical act, but with a question that plants doubt:

"Now the serpent was more cunning than any beast of the field which the LORD God had made. And he said to the woman, 'Has God indeed said, "You shall not eat of every tree of the garden"?'"

Genesis 3:1, NKJV

Notice the sequence: Satan didn't get Eve to sin in a single blow. First he had to talk to her. He had to plant the idea in her mind before she could act on it. Only after that conversation does the decisive moment arrive:

"So when the woman saw that the tree was good for food, that it was pleasant to the eyes, and a tree desirable to make one wise, she took of its fruit and ate. She also gave to her husband with her, and he ate."

Genesis 3:6, NKJV

The text says "when the woman saw…" — meaning, up until that point, Eve hadn't even set her attention on that tree in that particular way. She couldn't be tempted by something she hadn't considered. Only after the idea was planted in her mind through the conversation with the serpent did the temptation become real and concrete for her.

That's why Scripture insists so much on guarding what we listen to and where we place our attention:

"Take heed what you hear…"

Mark 4:24, NKJV

"Do not be deceived: 'Evil company corrupts good habits.'"

1 Corinthians 15:33, NKJV

"So then faith comes by hearing, and hearing by the word of God."

Romans 10:17, NKJV

The conclusion is as simple as it is powerful: if everything we meditated on were the Word of God, everything we produced would be faith. Doubt and unbelief don't come out of nowhere — they appear because, at some point, we meditated, even briefly, on something other than what God has said, and gave it enough room to "conceive" in our mind.

Abraham: the real key to his greatness

Here it's worth pausing on a figure who is often misunderstood: Abraham. He's known as "the father of faith" throughout biblical tradition, and it's almost automatically assumed that he had colossal faith, far greater than any of ours today. But Scripture suggests something different — and, in a sense, more attainable for us.

For context: God called Abraham (then named Abram) to leave Ur of the Chaldeans, his homeland, with the promise of making him the father of a great nation, even though he and his wife Sarah were already elderly and childless. Abraham spent decades waiting for that promise to be fulfilled. The book of Hebrews, reflecting on that long wait, says something revealing:

"And truly if they had called to mind that country from which they had come out, they would have had opportunity to return."

Hebrews 11:15, NKJV

In other words: the real possibility of Abraham returning to Ur, to his familiar homeland and comfort, was directly tied to whether he thought about it often. And, according to the text, he didn't. His greatness, then, didn't come from having more faith in absolute terms than we do — it came from having less doubt and unbelief, precisely because he disciplined what he set his mind on, day after day.

And it's important to note that this discipline wasn't just about avoiding the negative — it was, above all, about actively holding on to the positive. During the roughly 26 years between the original promise and its fulfillment, the one thing that occupied Abraham's mind was the promise itself: that his descendants would be as numerous as the stars of the sky and the sand on the seashore. It wasn't that Abraham fought doubt every day — it was that his mind was so occupied with the promise that doubt simply had nowhere to settle.

This is confirmed in how Scripture describes his response to a humanly impossible promise — having a child of his own at a hundred years old, with a barren wife:

"…who, contrary to hope, in hope believed, so that he became the father of many nations, according to what was spoken, 'So shall your descendants be.' And not being weak in faith, he did not consider his own body, already dead (since he was about a hundred years old), and the deadness of Sarah's womb."

Romans 4:18-19, NKJV

Notice the key phrase again: "he did not consider." Abraham didn't stop to dwell, over and over, on the impossibility of his natural situation — his advanced age, Sarah's barrenness. That's why doubt found no fertile ground to grow and multiply in his mind.

The same pattern repeats in the hardest episode of his entire life: when God asked him to offer his son Isaac, the long-awaited son of promise, as a sacrifice.

"Now it came to pass after these things that God tested Abraham, and said to him… 'Take now your son, your only son Isaac, whom you love, and go to the land of Moriah, and offer him there as a burnt offering.'"

Genesis 22:1-2, NKJV, abridged

Isaac, by then a young man of about seventeen, went up the mountain with his father, carrying the wood for the sacrifice, not knowing he himself would be the offering. At one point, he asked:

"'Look, the fire and the wood, but where is the lamb for a burnt offering?' And Abraham said, 'My son, God will provide for Himself the lamb for a burnt offering.'"

Genesis 22:7-8, NKJV

The account in Genesis records no complaint, no dragging of feet, not a single word of resistance from Abraham. Hebrews gives us the key to that astonishing steadiness:

"By faith Abraham, when he was tested, offered up Isaac, and he who had received the promises offered up his only begotten son… concluding that God was able to raise him up, even from the dead, from which he also received him in a figurative sense."

Hebrews 11:17-19, NKJV

Abraham simply never considered, never stopped to picture in detail, his son permanently dead and lost. His mind never settled into that scenario. And that's why he wasn't tempted to disobey or to negotiate with God.

It's worth pointing out one more thing, so this example doesn't stay only an admirable but distant feat: Abraham lived under a lesser covenant than the one we have today as those born again in Christ. If he, with less grace and less power available than we have, could discipline his mind to that degree, then how much more can we — who have more grace, not less — aspire to the same.

The law that governs all of this

What we've seen so far isn't chance or coincidence — it's a spiritual law, as consistent and predictable as any physical law, like gravity:

"For as he thinks in his heart, so is he."

Proverbs 23:7, NKJV

"Do not be deceived, God is not mocked; for whatever a man sows, that he will also reap."

Galatians 6:7, NKJV

"…as His divine power has given to us all things that pertain to life and godliness, through the knowledge of Him who called us by glory and virtue."

2 Peter 1:3, NKJV

Who we are today — spiritually speaking — is, to a large degree, the harvest of what we have sown our attention on over time, whether consciously or without realizing it.

It doesn't have to be sin

There's an important nuance worth clarifying here, because it's easy to assume this principle only applies to obviously bad things — clear sins, destructive behaviors. But Scripture frames it in a much broader way:

"The lamp of the body is the eye. If therefore your eye is good, your whole body will be full of light."

Matthew 6:22, NKJV

In other words: if you fill yourself with the Word of God, what comes out of you will be the Word of God. But the same principle works in reverse with anything that fills that space — and it doesn't have to be sinful. It can simply be anything else that isn't God.

Think of social media, for example. There's nothing inherently sinful about using it. But it's easy for it to absorb an enormous amount of a person's free time and available attention, day after day — and that time and attention have to come from somewhere. Often, without the person even noticing, it becomes a kind of passive indoctrination: hours spent absorbing the tone, priorities, and values of others, while the time meant for meditating on the things of God shrinks to almost nothing. It's not sin. But it is, without question, where the attention is going — and therefore, what will end up shaping the heart.

Sports are another good example. It's an industry that moves billions of dollars, deliberately designed to capture the public's attention — and it does so extraordinarily well. There's nothing wrong with enjoying a game. But when that interest crosses the line into obsession — constantly checking scores, following every detail of every team, letting a week's mood ride on a sports result — it becomes, in practice, the same mechanism as an addiction: it occupies the space of sustained attention that could otherwise be available for the things of God.

None of these examples is, by itself, a sin that needs to be confessed. That's exactly the point: something doesn't need to be "bad" for it to end up hardening the heart toward God. It just needs to consistently occupy the space of attention that would otherwise be available to consider God. The issue isn't so much what should be avoided, but, much more simply, where your available attention is going each day.

The good news: you can control your mind

At this point, it's easy to feel like this sounds overwhelming, almost impossible to fully manage. But here's a freeing truth, written precisely to counter that feeling of helplessness:

"For the weapons of our warfare are not carnal but mighty in God for pulling down strongholds, casting down arguments and every high thing that exalts itself against the knowledge of God, bringing every thought into captivity to the obedience of Christ."

2 Corinthians 10:4-5, NKJV

Think about how many times you've managed to keep your mind occupied, for an entire day, with a worry — a money problem, a tense relationship, a medical diagnosis that concerns you. No one had to teach you how to do that; it simply happened, almost automatically, because your attention got stuck there. If we're capable of sustaining that focus involuntarily, we're even more capable of training ourselves to sustain, deliberately and consciously, our attention on the Word of God. It's not a capacity reserved for "advanced spiritual people" or those with exceptional mental discipline: it's a decision available to anyone willing to practice it.

Point 3

The path to the cure

We've now covered what hardness of heart is and where it comes from. Now for the most important part of this journey: how it's reversed.

Focus on God, don't fight the world

Before getting into concrete steps, there's a distinction that changes the entire practical approach of this point — and it's possibly the single most important piece of this whole journey.

It's easy to think the solution to a hardened heart is a battle: identify what's bad, resist it, reject it, actively fight it every time it shows up. But it's worth considering this reflection, which points in a different direction:

Reflection

Many Christians wait until the battle begins, and then try to summon up faith, rebuke the devil, use the name of Jesus, and overcome the temptation. There's a better way: stop the temptation before it starts.

The "better way" isn't a more efficient rejection technique, or a more complete list of rules. It's a completely different change of direction. Instead of spending energy fighting evil head-on, the central instruction is much simpler:

It all starts by focusing your full attention on Jesus. Consider Jesus, and He will be the one dominating you.

Notice the exact logic of that sentence: it doesn't say "reject what's bad, and then Jesus will dominate." It says that simply considering Jesus — directing your attention toward Him, in a sustained way — is enough for Him, as a natural consequence, to end up dominating the heart. It's the same law we already saw in Point 2: whatever you focus on is what ends up governing. If the focus is on God, what happens with doubt, fear, or temptation isn't that you defeat them one by one in a conscious fight — it's that they simply stop having ground to grow in, because they're no longer receiving the attention they need to develop.

This also explains why, following that same line of thought, both sides of this dynamic are equally real and work by the same logic:

Reflection

If we ignore the Word of God, and don't spend time with the Lord, we harden toward Him. But on the other hand, if we ignore the enemy, we'll find it hard to hear his voice.

It's worth clarifying, in concrete terms, what this actually means in practice — so it doesn't stay an abstract idea. "Ignoring the Word" and "not spending time with the Lord" aren't two decorative phrases: directing attention toward God is seen, very concretely, in reading and meditating on Scripture; spending time with Him is seen in prayer and in actively seeking His direction for your life. These aren't isolated techniques or mechanical exercises — they are, at their core, the two ordinary ways of cultivating a personal relationship with God. Ignoring God's voice hardens the heart toward Him. But in the same way, ignoring the opposing voice — doubt, unbelief, the negativity around us — causes that voice to lose its power over us, without needing to fight it head-on. These aren't two wars to be fought at the same time. It's really a single decision of direction: where you point your time and attention most of the time.

"But the fruit of the Spirit is love, joy, peace, longsuffering, kindness, goodness, faithfulness, gentleness, self-control. Against such there is no law."

Galatians 5:22-23, NKJV

This — a life naturally marked by these qualities, without having to force them — is what should be normal for someone who has been born again. The other things (doubt, fear, destructive habits) only come in when we give them room and time to be "conceived" in the mind, day after day — not because there's some automatic pull toward them built into us.

An everyday example

To see this principle in action, imagine something as ordinary as this: you work somewhere where, almost daily, your coworkers openly criticize the authorities of your country — negative comments that have become routine, almost a running joke. You don't have to join in the criticism. But you don't need to prepare a speech or memorize a clever comeback for the moment, either. It's enough that, over time, you've meditated on — not just read once, but considered, thought over again, let sit — something as simple as this:

"Blessed is the man who walks not in the counsel of the ungodly, nor stands in the path of sinners, nor sits in the seat of the scornful."

Psalm 1:1, NKJV

Because then, when the moment comes, there's no need to invent anything under pressure. You can simply say, naturally, something like: "according to Romans 13:1-4, our leaders have been placed by God for our good, and that's why I've been praying for our government" — and go on with your day. Do you know what will happen with them? Either they repent, or at the very least they never bring it up in front of you again. Either way, you win.

There was no fight. There was no speech prepared in advance for the occasion, no memorization technique applied in the moment. What came out was simply what was already inside — the fruit of having calmly meditated on it long before, not of having read it once.

One important clarification before moving on: this is just one example among many possible ones, not a script to follow or a model for "what to say" in every argument. Most of the time, you won't even need to say anything — it will be enough for the Word, already settled in the mind beforehand, to hold up, on the inside, how you react. The central point of this whole journey isn't learning lines to defend yourself from the world. It's that when the heart has already been filled, over time, with sustained attention toward God — whether through meditating on His Word, or simply deciding not to let social media or sports take up every available minute of the day — what comes out of that person, in any situation, is exactly what was already inside.

Understanding why sometimes "faith doesn't work"

There's a very common moment in a life of faith that tends to cause confusion, and even a bit of guilt: you pray, you believe, and yet you don't see the result you expected. The typical reaction is to think, "I must have lacked faith." But it's worth taking a close look at a revealing episode in the Gospels.

Here's the context: Jesus had just come down from the Mount of Transfiguration — where He had appeared in glory before Peter, James, and John — and ran into a chaotic scene: a crowd surrounding His disciples, and a desperate man pushing his way to Jesus:

"Lord, have mercy on my son, for he is an epileptic and suffers severely; for he often falls into the fire and often into the water. So I brought him to Your disciples, but they could not cure him."

Matthew 17:15-16, NKJV

Jesus healed the boy immediately. But afterward, in private, the disciples — who had already been sent out before with authority to cast out spirits and heal diseases, and had done so successfully — asked Him why they hadn't been able to do it this time. Jesus's answer is the key to this whole point:

"Because of your unbelief…"

Matthew 17:20, NKJV

Notice He didn't say "for lack of faith" in the sense that they had no faith at all. He said "because of your unbelief" — a different word, pointing to something else. That distinction completely changes how we understand the problem. Faith and unbelief are not opposites that automatically cancel each other out: they can coexist in the same person, at the same time.

Scripture confirms this in several places. When Jairus, a synagogue ruler whose daughter was dying, received word that she had already died while he was on his way with Jesus, Jesus told him:

"Do not be afraid; only believe."

Mark 5:36, NKJV

Why would Jesus need to add "only," if faith already excluded, by definition, any fear or doubt? Precisely because both things — faith and fear, faith and unbelief — can be present at the same time in the heart of the same person, competing to control the final reaction.

In the parallel account of the tormented boy, as told in the Gospel of Mark, the father says something remarkably honest to Jesus:

"Lord, I believe; help my unbelief!"

Mark 9:24, NKJV

He's a man acknowledging, with no inner contradiction, that he had genuine faith and genuine unbelief at the same time. And in another teaching about faith that moves mountains, Jesus again points to this double possibility:

"For assuredly, I say to you, whoever says to this mountain, 'Be removed and be cast into the sea,' and does not doubt in his heart, but believes that those things he says will be done, he will have whatever he says."

Mark 11:23, NKJV

Again, the condition isn't just "believe" — it's believe without doubting. If doubting weren't a real, active possibility for a believer, that clarification would be unnecessary.

Two forces pulling in opposite directions

To picture this simply, imagine two teams of horses tied to the same heavy load, each pulling in the opposite direction with exactly the same force. The net result: the load doesn't move an inch, even though both teams are pulling with everything they have. That's often how our life of faith works: we release genuine, real faith when we pray, but that faith gets neutralized by an equally real and active unbelief we've never dealt with or even identified.

"For assuredly, I say to you, if you have faith as a mustard seed, you will say to this mountain, 'Move from here to there,' and it will move; and nothing will be impossible for you."

Matthew 17:20, NKJV

The faith we received at new birth is, by itself, enough for any miracle — even compared to something as tiny as a mustard seed. The problem is almost never "I need more faith" — it's "I have unresolved unbelief that's canceling out, in practice, the faith I already have."

"If any of you lacks wisdom, let him ask of God… But let him ask in faith, with no doubting, for he who doubts is like a wave of the sea driven and tossed by the wind. For let not that man suppose that he will receive anything from the Lord; he is a double-minded man, unstable in all his ways."

James 1:5-8, NKJV

The good news within this truth is that a single verse, fully believed with no active unbelief contradicting it, is enough to sustain an entire life of faith:

"But He was wounded for our transgressions, He was bruised for our iniquities; the chastisement for our peace was upon Him, and by His stripes we are healed."

Isaiah 53:5, NKJV

You don't need a thousand memorized Bible promises if that quiet, underlying unbelief hasn't first been identified and dealt with.

Your senses can be trained too

One last piece of this puzzle has to do with our physical senses — what we see, hear, and feel in the body. Unlike the sensitivity of the heart (which responds naturally and automatically to what we consider), the senses and the body do require active training, and often a real fight:

"But solid food belongs to those who are of full age, that is, those who by reason of use have their senses exercised to discern both good and evil."

Hebrews 5:14, NKJV

This explains why the disciples, despite having genuine faith and having previously been commissioned with real authority, failed in front of the tormented boy: it wasn't deliberate disobedience — it was that their senses — what they saw in front of them, the boy's physical convulsions, the foaming, the screaming — weren't aligned with or submitted to what their spirit already knew from the Word and from prior experience. Seeing the physical manifestation of the problem, they related more to the natural than to faith. It's the exact same pattern as Mark 6:52 — the hardened heart facing the water and the storm — just in a different setting, with different people.

It's worth remembering, at this point, that an unrenewed mind naturally leans toward this resistance:

"Because the carnal mind is enmity against God; for it is not subject to the law of God, nor indeed can be."

Romans 8:7, NKJV

That's why training the senses and the body does require constant, deliberate exercise — unlike the heart, which grows sensitive as a natural consequence of where we look, the body and its senses have been shaped by years of habit, and they don't give up that habit without a fight.

So what are prayer and fasting for?

We come to the last piece of the path, and perhaps the most misunderstood one. Going back to the episode of the tormented boy, when Jesus explained privately why the disciples hadn't been able to free him, He added one more phrase, recorded in Mark's account:

"This kind can come out by nothing but prayer and fasting."

Mark 9:29, NKJV

It's easy to read this and think that prayer and fasting are some kind of "stronger weapon" reserved for especially difficult spiritual cases — as if some problems needed to "convince" God more, or "pressure" the enemy more until he backs down. But it's worth making an important clarification that changes the whole meaning of the phrase: prayer and fasting do not move God or the devil. God already gave, in advance, all the authority and power His followers need:

"Behold, I give you the authority to trample on serpents and scorpions, and over all the power of the enemy, and nothing shall by any means hurt you."

Luke 10:19, NKJV

So what do prayer and fasting actually do, if they don't move God? They bring the senses and the flesh — which do need a real fight, as we just saw — under control, training them to respond to the spirit, instead of the other way around, which is what happens by default. They help so that when the mind sees something that contradicts what the Word already says, it doesn't automatically react with unbelief, but stays anchored to what God already said, even in the middle of physical evidence that seems to contradict it.

Jesus Himself, after forty days of fasting in the wilderness, being tempted by Satan to turn stones into bread, responded by leaning precisely on the strength of the Word over any immediate physical need:

"It is written, 'Man shall not live by bread alone, but by every word that proceeds from the mouth of God.'"

Matthew 4:4, NKJV

"For they are life to those who find them, and health to all their flesh."

Proverbs 4:22, NKJV

You already have everything you need

And there's a foundational truth, maybe the most freeing one in this whole journey, that holds up everything we've covered: it's not about getting "more" from God through prayer and fasting — it's about learning to release and activate what was already received the moment you were born again — that is, the moment a person receives Jesus as Lord and Savior.

"For where your treasure is, there your heart will be also."

Matthew 6:21, NKJV

When you were born again, you received the same measure of faith Jesus Himself had:

"For I say, through the grace given to me, to everyone who is among you, not to think of himself more highly than he ought to think, but to think soberly, as God has dealt to each one a measure of faith."

Romans 12:3, NKJV

"I have been crucified with Christ; it is no longer I who live, but Christ lives in me; and the life which I now live in the flesh I live by faith in the Son of God, who loved me and gave Himself for me."

Galatians 2:20, NKJV

There's no need to ask God for "more" faith, as if He were holding something back. What's needed, rather, is to identify and remove the specific unbelief that's blocking what's already available.

Closing

Bringing this journey to a close

If we had to sum up this whole path in one idea, it would be this:

The heart grows sensitive, naturally and without needing to fight, toward whatever it sustains its attention on — and it hardens toward whatever it neglects, even something it has personally lived through.

It's not a divine punishment, nor a condition reserved for "bad people," nor a battle to be won through sheer resistance. It's a spiritual dynamic that operates in everyone, all the time, according to where we choose to direct our attention day by day.

The good news is twofold. First, this condition can be reversed — not over twenty or thirty years of effort, but from the very moment you begin redirecting your attention toward God in a sustained way. Second, you don't need "more" of God to do it: it's all already been given from the new birth. All that's left is to consider Him — and let Him, as a natural consequence, be the one who ends up dominating the heart.
